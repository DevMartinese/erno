Pattern

Write a function of a cubie's position and the engine paints a puzzle with it. Then scramble it and bring the pattern back, by hand or by writing the sequence. Two puzzles stacked: one you write, one you turn.

This page is not part of the library. It is one file of glue over the public API, kept here to show what the engine is for.

Write

Your function runs once per sticker, not per cubie: a corner hears it three times, once for each face it shows on. x, y, z are the cubie's place on the lattice and n how far that lattice reaches; face is the letter of the face this sticker shows on, row and col its place in that face's grid, and kind how many stickers its cubie wears: 1 centre, 2 edge, 3 corner.

A cube already names six colours, and not red and blue: U, R, F, D, L, B, the same alphabet its moves are written in. Returning a face letter returns its colour, so return face is the solved cube, eleven characters, and return kind == 3 ? 0 : face is the solved cube with its corners inked out. Numbers 0 to 7 reach a wider palette for the pictures six face colours cannot make.

Nothing here is a special mode. paint is an ordinary option on an ordinary cube, so what comes out is a working puzzle and not a picture of one.

Bodies

One body is a cube and every turn is open. Weld a second on and most of them stop existing: the class underneath changes from Cube to Fused, the keypad greys out, and the code below rewrites itself. Put one off the shared lattice and the library refuses it and says why.

Stamp

The first mode where the pattern is handed to you. Write lets you invent a picture; here one arrives - from the album, or grown from a seed anyone can share - your cube is solid blank, and you recreate it sticker by sticker: pick a colour, click a sticker. The polygons were always clickable; every one carries its face and index.

A cube has six faces and a picture shows three, so there are two views: the top reads U R F, the belly reads D L B, and the target flips with your canvas, so what you are copying is always what you are looking at. Scored in brushstrokes against the pattern's own minimum: every stroke beyond it was a stroke taken back.

Assemble

The cube piece by piece. The pattern arrives, the pieces are dealt from a shuffled bin, and for each you choose a slot and a spin. Centres come pre-placed, because on the real thing they are bolted to the core: take a 3×3 apart and the six centres stay on the spider.

Two crowns wait at the end. Matching the picture is one. The other is the law: a blind reassembly of a 3×3 is solvable one time in twelve (a factor three for corner twist, a two for edge flip, a two for parity), so you can build the perfect picture and an impossible cube, and the judge will tell you which you built, and why.

Solve

The goal is the cube you just wrote, at rest. That matters more than it sounds: the target is reachable because the puzzle was standing on it a moment ago, so no solver is needed and no impossible pattern can be asked for.

The move buttons ask the puzzle what is available rather than assuming. On a plain cube everything always is. Point the same code at a welded or bandaged puzzle and it stops being true, which is why the question is asked at all.

The buttons turn one face; the box below turns a sentence, in the notation cubers already write. [R, U] is a commutator, [R: U] a conjugate, (R U)3 a repeat, and you can name things, because cubers name their algorithms: let sexy = R U R' U'; (sexy)6. What it prints back is not the move list but what the sequence does, in the names cubers use: UB → RF → UR says which pieces travel together, and the order says how many repeats bring it home. A commutator moving three pieces and nothing else is the whole reason they are taught, and it is one line here rather than a lecture.

Target

Or break the laws. Turning can never make a position no sequence reaches; a thumb can. Twist one corner and the pattern is gone for good, and the status line names the law that says so. 


        


        

      

      

        

      

    


    

      

        
Script

        

          The third verb. You could paint by function and turn by hand or by
          sentence; this turns by function: your code reads the cube,
          decides, and calls turn(), against the same board and
          target the keypad plays. The control flow is JavaScript's, because
          inventing loops would be inventing a worse JavaScript.
        

        

          The nouns are the cube's own. turn(seq) takes the whole
          algebra, macros included. at("UFR") answers which piece
          stands where the UFR corner rests, in its home letters.
          face("U") reads the nine letters showing on a face.
          solved() and distance() judge against the
          target, and moves() counts what you have spent. Every
          call burns fuel, so a runaway loop that keeps asking dies with a
          message; one that never asks cannot be stopped from here, so save
          before you gamble.
        

        

          Scored twice, because they are two different virtues: fewest
          characters is insight about the writing, fewest moves is insight
          about the cube.

The code

        

          Not an example written once and left to rot beside the thing it
          describes. This is generated from the same state the page is
          drawing from, so it cannot drift: change the puzzle and the
          constructor changes, weld two bodies and the bodies appear, turn a
          face and the move shows up, type a sequence and its permutation is
          printed under it.
        

        

          It is meant to run. Put it in a file beside the library and you get
          the puzzle above, in the position above. There is a test that
          pastes it into node and checks that it does.
